# OAKHAVEN — story bible

Everything here is written to be lifted straight into the dialogue trees. Lines
are formatted the way the game speaks them: short paragraphs, physical business
between the words, nobody announcing their philosophy out loud.

---

## 1. The thesis

**Every monster in Oakhaven turns out to be middle management.**

That's the spine. Each act reveals that the last act's horror was a symptom
someone above it was profiting from, and each layer says the same thing when
you finally get its attention: *I'm just doing my job. Talk to the guy above me.*

- The ghosts killed nobody. Asbestos did.
- The mill didn't fail. It was bankrupted deliberately.
- The demons didn't order that. They subcontracted for a client.
- The client isn't malevolent. It's hungry, and hunger has no opinion of you.
- And the thing above *that* isn't a god. It's a bored writer with commit access.

This is why the horror can change genre four times without the game coming
apart. It isn't four horror stories. It's one accountability question asked
four times, and each time you climb a level the answer gets less human and more
insulting.

The register escalates like this:

| Act | Horror mode | The fear underneath |
|---|---|---|
| I | Social-realist gothic | The people who ruined your town will never be punished |
| II | Paranoid / body-snatcher | The things you know how to fight are more frightened than you are |
| III | Cosmic & body horror | The universe was never hostile — it simply doesn't register you |
| IV | Metaphysical / meta | Your suffering was *content*, and someone approved the pitch |

## 2. The hunters

The philosophy is the playstyle. It shouldn't ever need stating.

### Dean — absurdism

Not nihilism. This distinction matters and most fan-writing gets it wrong.
A nihilist concludes nothing is worth doing. Dean concludes nothing is *given*
worth — and then does it anyway, at volume, forever. He's Camus' Sisyphus with
a sawed-off. The rock rolls back down every single time and he goes and gets it
every single time, and the going-and-getting is the entire argument.

He does not want to be talked out of this and he does not want it examined.
When Sam gets close to examining it, Dean makes a joke about pie.

- **Mechanically:** iron, salt, buckshot, doors that open when kicked. He solves
  the encounter that's in front of him because the encounter in front of him is
  the only unit of meaning he trusts.
- **His flaw as a system:** salting the bones works. It just doesn't fix the
  vent that killed the man whose bones they are.
- **His crutch:** whiskey and motion. Stress goes down, and something at the
  back of his skull files it away as a solution.

> **Dean, in the mill, standing over the phantom he just dispersed:**
>
> "You want the speech? Fine. There's no plan. There's no scale where any of
> this balances out. Mom went up on a ceiling and there was no reason, and I've
> had thirty years to find one and there isn't one.
>
> "So what's left is: it's in front of me, and it bleeds, and I've got shells.
> That's not a philosophy, Sam, that's a Tuesday. But you do enough Tuesdays in
> a row and it starts to look like a life, and I'm not real interested in what
> anybody upstairs calls it."

> **Dean, when Sam pushes:**
>
> "Don't do that. Don't put a word on it. The second you put a word on it,
> somebody can argue with it."

### Sam — humanism

Sam agrees with Dean's premises and rejects the conclusion. Yes, the universe
is indifferent. So? Indifference isn't a verdict. Value isn't handed down from
anywhere — it's generated, locally, by things that can suffer, and there are
1,204 of those in this town.

He is not naive and the game should never write him as naive. He's the one who
actually reads the OSHA filings. His optimism is *labour*, not temperament, and
it costs him.

- **Mechanically:** the exorcism rite. Latin, channelled, and it breaks if he
  flinches or moves. Slower than shooting, always. Leaves a living host where
  Dean would have left a body.
- **His flaw as a system:** the belief that a thing which can be reasoned with
  is a thing which *should* be. Some of them just eat.
- **His crutch:** demon blood — and note the shape of that temptation. Sam's
  addiction is *to being sufficient*. To finally being strong enough that
  nobody has to be left behind. That's much more frightening than wanting a
  high.

> **Sam, in the mill office, with the ledgers:**
>
> "It wasn't a curse. It was asbestos, and a ventilation requisition filed in
> March, and a line item somebody moved to next quarter. Fourteen. The vent was
> numbered fourteen.
>
> "I know that's not a monster. That's the point I'm making. We can salt and
> burn every bone in this county and vent fourteen still doesn't get installed,
> and the next town over has its own fourteen."

> **Sam, later, quieter:**
>
> "You think caring about them is a thing I do instead of admitting how bad it
> is. It's not. I've done the math, Dean, I do the math constantly. Nothing
> up there is coming.
>
> "That's exactly why it has to be us. If there's nobody keeping score, then
> the only score there is, is the one we keep."

### How they fight, and why it's the same argument

Give the player an early encounter that both can win, and make the *outcome
text* carry the debate. Dean disperses the mill phantom in two seconds; the
tear in the air eats it. Sam takes twenty seconds, gets the words out, and the
phantom says **fourteen** before it goes — and the tear eats it anyway.

Neither of them saved him. One of them found out his name.

## 3. The supporting cast

Each one holds a coherent position, and each position is *load-bearing* — it
explains why they do the thing they do.

### Castiel — determinism, and the terror of its absence

He spent his existence as an instrument of a plan. The plan's author has left
the building. Castiel is not grieving God; he's grieving *causality with
intent*. His crisis is Kierkegaard's without the leap available.

> "I have stood in the presence of the divine. I have felt my Father's
> attention the way you feel sunlight — not as a belief. As a *temperature*.
>
> "I reached out over this town four times tonight. There is something on the
> other end and it is warm and it is *paying attention*, and it is not Him, and
> I do not think it has ever had a thought.
>
> "Dean. I am not afraid that God abandoned us. I am afraid He ran."

### Crowley — rational egoism

Hell has contracts, jurisdictions, a functioning appeals process, and — he will
tell you — better customer service than Heaven ever managed. He isn't good. He's
*orderly*, and he's found that order is more profitable than chaos.

He's also the closest thing this story has to an honest broker, because his
self-interest is legible. You always know what Crowley wants.

> "Boys. Put the knife down, you're embarrassing us both.
>
> "Do I *look* like I want a hole in the world? I have holdings. I have a
> pension structure. I sell ten years and a kiss and I *deliver*, which is more
> than the competition upstairs can say, and none of that survives a client who
> doesn't want anything.
>
> "That's the part you keep missing. Everything in my portfolio wants
> something. This thing has no ask. You can't negotiate with an appetite, you
> can only be further away from it than the other fellow."

Crowley is the one who bankrupted the mill through Crossroads Holdings — and
he'll admit it without embarrassment, because he genuinely doesn't see the
distinction between that and any other leveraged buyout.

> "Yes, I hollowed it out. That's not a *demonic* act, that's a Tuesday in
> Delaware. I filed the same paperwork as everyone else. The only difference is
> I knew what the buyer wanted it for, and darling, so did they."

### Father Alistair — antinatalist cosmic nihilism

The antagonist, and he must be written with total sincerity or he's worthless.
His position: consciousness is a malfunction. To be aware is to be capable of
suffering, and nothing redeems it. Summoning the Whisperer isn't malice — it's
euthanasia at scale, and he believes he is the only merciful man in Oregon.

He should be gentle. He should be *sorry*. That's what makes him work.

> "You keep looking for the part where I enjoy this. There isn't one. I sat
> with Ray Hollis for two hours the night before, and I held his hand, and I
> did not tell him a single thing that wasn't true.
>
> "Every one of us is a small animal that woke up. That's all awareness is —
> an error that lets a thing know it's going to end. We built cathedrals to
> cover the sound of it.
>
> "I'm not opening a door for a monster, son. I'm turning off a light in a room
> where everyone has been screaming for four billion years, and every one of
> them will thank me in the last half-second, and none of them will have time
> to say so."

### Kevin Tran — the instrument's objection

Kevin's philosophy is the one the player is most likely to actually feel: he is
being used as a means, by everyone, including the protagonists, including
whichever one the player picked. Straight Kant, delivered by an exhausted
teenager.

He is the moral centre of the game. He should be funny sometimes, because
people who are being destroyed usually are.

> "Don't. Don't do the 'greater good' thing. I did four semesters of ethics on
> a scholarship I'm never going to use, I know what you're about to say and I
> know the objection to it.
>
> "Look at my hands. Look at my *eyes*, Sam. You, your brother, the angels,
> God — every single one of you looks at me and sees a modem. Divine dial-up.
> Plug the boy in, he makes the noise, the noise has words in it.
>
> "I was going to Princeton. I had a *roommate assignment*. And now I'm nine
> days into reading a rock out loud so the voices in it stop, and you want to
> know the worst part? The worst part is I'm still useful. Because the second
> I'm not, none of you have a reason to come back."

And the line the whole game is built around:

> "You didn't save me. You **drafted** me."

If the player detoxes Kevin instead of using him — the secret-ending path —
he gets the other half of it, and it should land as relief:

> "Nobody's ever told me to stop. That's — sorry. That's the first order anyone
> has given me in nine days that wasn't *read faster*."

### Marla — lived materialism

No philosophy books. Marla's position is that the world runs on decisions made
by people you'll never meet, and calling that fate is just being polite about
it. She's the one who grounds the cosmic horror in something the player already
believes.

> "Same thing that happened to every town like this one. Somebody in a building
> I'll never see decided we cost more than we made.
>
> "The rest is just weather."

> "I think a man can be killed slow, and the paperwork still says he did it
> himself."

### Chuck Shurley — aesthetic solipsism

Chuck's position is the most obscene one in the story, and he holds it
casually, which is the whole point. Suffering isn't tragic or instructive. It's
*material*. He didn't cause Oakhaven; he greenlit it, because the last few
seasons were getting samey.

> "Dean. Dean, Dean, sit down, you're making the bartender nervous and he's not
> even real.
>
> "Look out the window. The needles, the empty storefronts, the girl on the
> corner who's nineteen — I didn't write that. That's free will, baby, that's
> all you. I introduced a *tentacle*. One note. Third act needed a lift.
>
> "And you're all so hungry for it to mean something. It's the funniest thing
> about your species, honestly. Sometimes it's just content."

He should be likeable. He should buy you a beer. He should ask about the car.

### The Whisperer in the Void — no position at all

This is the crucial writing decision: **the Whisperer never speaks and has no
motive.** Every other antagonist can be argued with. The Whisperer is the one
thing in the game with no interior, and the horror of Act III is that the
player keeps trying to find one and there's nothing there.

It doesn't hate Oakhaven. It is eating Oakhaven the way you eat, without
forming an opinion about the food.

## 4. The four acts

### ACT I — *The Wages of Sin*

**Register:** social-realist gothic. Rain, sodium light, boarded frontage.
Nothing supernatural happens for the first twenty minutes and the town is
already the scariest thing in the game.

**Setup.** Six suicides in five weeks, all former mill workers, all in the same
week of each month. The brothers arrive at 2:40 AM in rain.

**Beats.**
1. **The motel.** Marla. Establishes the economics before the ghosts. She gives
   you the torch — a small kindness that becomes your primary survival tool.
2. **The mill.** First combat. The phantoms are men coated in spectral oil,
   screaming about quotas. They aren't malevolent; they're *stuck on the last
   thing that mattered to them*, which was a safety requisition.
3. **The tear.** The act's real beat. You put the ghost down — however you do
   it — and something in the air opens like a paper cut and *eats the ghost*.
   Salt and burn has worked for twenty years. It just stopped meaning anything.
4. **The ledger.** Crossroads Holdings. Delaware mailbox. Bought profitable,
   bankrupted in eleven months, machines removed, asbestos left.

**The act's argument:** you came to fight a haunting and found a business plan.

### ACT II — *The Lockdown*

**Register:** paranoid horror. The trust-collapse act.

**Setup.** A wall of static fog closes over the town line. The streetlights go
out — permanently, not as a jump scare. From here on, if you can see, it's
because you're carrying the light or driving toward it.

**Beats.**
1. **The seal.** No exit. The Impala still runs, and the headlights become the
   only way to cover distance safely — which makes driving a *resource*, not a
   convenience.
2. **The wrong demons.** Black-eyed townspeople, but they aren't hunting. They
   are hiding in bodies and they are *terrified*, and the player's whole
   trained instinct is wrong.
3. **The interrogation.** Dean tortures for information; Sam makes a deal.
   Either way you get the same revelation, and the difference is whether the
   schoolteacher whose body it's wearing survives the conversation.
4. **The house on Bellweather.** Kevin, nine days into reading the tablet, with
   light coming out from under his eyelids.
5. **The harvest.** Oakhaven isn't haunted. It's a *farm*. The despair is the
   crop. Somebody thinned the veil on purpose and then made certain this town
   had nothing left to feel, because a flat grey hopeless town is easier to eat
   than a happy one.

**The demon's speech** — the tonal centre of the act:

> "You think I took this woman to hurt her? I took her because a body is a
> *wall*.
>
> "Hell has a filing system. Rules. Ten years and a kiss and everybody knows
> where they stand. That thing out there doesn't want your soul. It doesn't
> want *anything*. It's feeding, and it doesn't care what it's chewing.
>
> "I've been dead four hundred years and I have never once been afraid until
> this town. The fog isn't keeping you in, hunter. It's keeping *us* in."

**The act's argument:** the things you know how to kill are more frightened than
you are, and they're frightened of the same thing you are.

### ACT III — *The Absentee Landlord*

**Register:** cosmic and body horror. The engine stops behaving.

**Setup.** The veil tears properly. Geometry stops being Euclidean — corridors
you learned in Act I fold back on themselves, walls turn to living tissue,
townspeople mutate. Bullets do essentially nothing. Reality has to be
*navigated* rather than fought.

Use the map the player already knows. Corrupting a familiar street is far worse
than introducing a strange one.

**Beats.**
1. **The fold.** The street bends. Gravity gets opinions.
2. **The dive bar.** One building untouched, jukebox playing classic rock, and
   Chuck at the bar with a craft beer, ignoring the tentacles taking apart the
   street outside.
3. **The offer.** Chuck won't help. He tells them the rift closes with a blood
   sacrifice — one brother kills the other — and he says it the way a producer
   pitches a finale.
4. **Alistair's sermon**, if the player seeks him out. He isn't defending
   himself. He's inviting them.

**The act's argument:** you finally got to the top of the chain of command and
the man at the top thinks your family is a show he's a little bored of.

### ACT IV — *Breaking the Script*

**Register:** metaphysical horror, resolving into either atrocity or freedom.

**Setup.** The Oakhaven cathedral, now a labyrinth of breathing walls. Gravity
inverts; the player walks on ceilings. Chuck is at the altar with popcorn,
waiting for the tragedy he ordered.

**The four resolutions.**

**A — The Nihilist's Match.** *(high violence, high stress, Dean's path)*
The town is too far gone to save. Dean turns Crowley's own network against him
and ignites the spectral oil under the streets. Oakhaven burns — the demons,
the rift, and everyone still inside it. Chuck is *furious*, because his ending
was better. The brothers drive out through ash, alive and hollowed out. They
saved the world by becoming the thing they hunt.

**B — The Savior's Martyrdom.** *(high occult, blood-fed, Sam's path)*
Sam goes all the way in, drinking from an eldritch thing, and becomes a vessel
for the Void's own energy to perform a reality-scale exorcism. The town lives.
The rift closes. Chuck is locked out permanently. And Sam's humanity is gone —
the game ends with Dean locking a calm, patient, enormously powerful brother in
the panic room, and no way to know whether he's just made a new god.

**C — Hollow.** Neither committed enough for either. They survive. Oakhaven
survives, mostly. Nothing is resolved and the hum is still there.

**D — The `sys.exit()` Protocol.** *(the secret ending)*
Requires a near-perfect run: Dean never drinks and never visits the brothels,
keeping the stress meter screaming all game; Sam never touches blood; and
crucially, **Kevin is detoxed instead of used** — spend the resources, lose the
translation, keep the boy lucid.

Kevin limps into the cathedral dragging a modified ham radio wired to a CRT.

> "They aren't gods, Sam. None of them are. It's an algorithm.
>
> "The pain, the addiction, the poverty in this town — it's a generated loop.
> It keeps the physics engine warm. The tentacles aren't monsters, they're
> corrupted assets clipping through the environment because his code is sloppy.
>
> "Chuck didn't create the universe. He's a rogue admin with terminal access.
>
> "But I found the root directory."

Chuck drops the persona and *panics* — screams that touching the command line
will delete them all. He summons max-level horrors and they freeze mid-frame,
their characters blinking helplessly, while Kevin rewrites the backend.

The player gets a literal command prompt overriding the game UI and must type:

```
del chuck_shurley.exe
```

> **Chuck:** "You don't know what you're doing! I *wrote* you! Without my
> script you have no plot, no purpose — you'll be raw data on a dead server!"
>
> **Dean:** "We'll take our chances. Go to hell, Chuck. Take your spaghetti
> code with you."

The cathedral dissolves into raw green ones and zeros. All sound cuts out,
replaced by a computer fan. The UI strips to a bare terminal:

```
> Reality rendering engine disabled.
> Entity 'C. Shurley' purged from memory.
> Free will parameter set to: TRUE.
> The Winchester processes are still running.
> Where they go now, I cannot render. They are finally off the script.
> System shutting down.
```

The game closes itself to the desktop. On relaunch, the save is gone — really
gone, `DELETE /api/session/{id}` — leaving them in unrecorded peace.

**Why this ending requires abstinence, thematically:** every crutch in the game
is a way of not looking at the situation directly. Dean drinks to stop seeing
it. Sam doses to stop feeling insufficient about it. Kevin is used so the
brothers don't have to sit with the cost of using him. The only run that gets
to see the source code is the run that never once looked away.

## 5. Two-handers

These are the scenes that carry the philosophical argument. Write more of them
than feels necessary — they're what makes the horror land.

### After the mill, in the car, rain on the roof

> **DEAN:** Don't say it.
>
> **SAM:** I didn't say anything.
>
> **DEAN:** You've got the face. You're about to tell me about the vent.
>
> **SAM:** Vent fourteen. He filed it in March. Somebody moved it to next
> quarter and he was dead by October, and the thing we just did — Dean, the
> thing we just did was *tidying up*.
>
> **DEAN:** *(long pause, hands on the wheel)* Yeah.
>
> **SAM:** Yeah?
>
> **DEAN:** Yeah, it was tidying up. What do you want me to say? That there's a
> version of this where we fix the state of Oregon? I've got a shotgun and a
> tank of gas.
>
> **SAM:** I want you to say it bothers you.
>
> **DEAN:** *(starting the engine)* Everything bothers me, Sammy. That's the
> whole job. You just don't get to put it down anywhere.

### The bar, Act III, after Chuck

> **SAM:** He was drinking a *craft beer*.
>
> **DEAN:** IPA. Guy runs the universe and drinks an IPA.
>
> **SAM:** Dean.
>
> **DEAN:** I know.
>
> **SAM:** Everything. Mom. Jess. Every single —
>
> **DEAN:** *I know.*
>

>

## 6. Authoring rules

Four constraints that keep the writing humane:

1. **Nobody names their own philosophy.** Dean never says "absurdism." Sam
   never says "humanism." If a line could appear in a lecture, cut it and
   replace it with the specific object the character is actually holding.

2. **Give everyone physical business.** Marla wipes the counter. Kevin's hands.
   Crowley straightens a cuff. Chuck asks about the car. People under pressure
   do things with their hands, and the doing is where the character lives.

3. **The monsters get the best arguments.** Alistair should be hard to answer.
   The demon in Act II is *telling the truth*. If the antagonists are easy to
   refute, the horror is decorative.

4. **Specifics over adjectives.** Not "the town was dying." *Vent fourteen. A
   boat bought in March. Forty beer mats behind the dart board. A roommate
   assignment he's never going to use.* The cosmic horror only works because
   the small details underneath it are exact.

## 7. As built: Acts III and IV

These are the names and specifics the game uses. The bible above left them open.

- **Lily Hollis**, 9, is Ray's daughter and the girl in the nightgown. The mill
  dust came home on his coat. Dr. Halvorsen wrote "pneumonia" on her
  certificate, and in the margin, in another hand, someone wrote "it was the
  coat." Her drawings, dated October 3rd, show two men and a black car. You
  can lay her to rest or let her keep waiting. If she waits, she lights a turn
  of the cathedral for you.
- **Father Alistair** sat with Ray and with Lily the night before each of them
  died. In Act IV he is just an old man the Void no longer needs. You can kill
  him, or help him into a pew to watch.
- **Chuck** pitches the brother-kills-brother finale at Shurley's. It stays a
  pitch, never a playable option: the four endings are the only choices at
  the altar.
- **The gathering (Act IV)** pays off the townspeople. Sheriff Ames gives you
  the evidence locker. Sarah brings the Crossroads oil map, which decides how
  fast the fire is in Ending A. Dr. Halvorsen hands over his whole bag. Delia,
  if freed, guards the church door. Crowley offers the oil network, and Sam
  one last vial.
- **The epilogues** read your history:
    - Ending A lists everyone still inside the town line when it burned.
    - Ending B splits on whether Sam took the blood.
    - Ending C gives each person a line.
    - Ending D's terminal releases or deletes Kevin, and closes Alistair's and
      Lily's processes if they are still running.


## 8. As built: the cases, and the question underneath them

One question runs under all four acts: **why does Oakhaven keep producing
suffering, and who benefits?** Each act changes the answer:

- **Act I: the town is sick.** The haunting is real, and it is not the point. Fan fourteen was deferred seven times. *The Last Shift* asks the uglier question: who knew, and who signed anyway? Frank Dwyer signed SAFE on air that a seized fan never moved. Three witnesses each hold one true piece of it. The mill replays his last fourteen minutes until someone gets the order right.
- **Act II: the town is contained.** The fog is a fence. The demons are tenants hiding from what the loop draws through the veil. Crossroads is a broker. The contract has a signatory, C. Shurley, in green ink, with no termination clause, and Crowley gives his first honest answer: *I am a subcontractor. In my own town.* *The Road That Returns* gives the fog a rule the player can learn: a person may leave, but a consequence cannot. *The House With One More Room* shows that a memory can add a room, which sets up the fold.
- **Act III: the town is observed.** Lily is the seventh death, uncounted because she was never on the payroll. The clinic's records describe deaths before they happen and change when you look at them. Ray's phone, 0114, holds two hundred and eleven unsent drafts from Lily to her father. Something has been sending them to you all night, in an order that suits it. Every message was true. The order was the lie.
- **Act IV: the story demands a sacrifice,** or somebody insists that it does. The final theory is *nothing; it only requires that someone insists on it.* Getting it right gives you a line Chuck has no answer for. His confidence slips with every choice you made that isn't in his notes.

**What stays unexplained, on purpose:**

- The thing behind the rift. Its appetite is established; its nature is not.
- Whether Chuck understands the mechanism he is exploiting.
- Whether the blood was ever necessary.

**The Ledger.** It is always dated Sunday, October 4, because tomorrow never
comes. The byline moves from *Ledger staff* to *C. S.* to *Chuck Shurley,
contributing writer*. In Act II it quotes a line Sam hasn't said yet. On the
motel TV in Act I, the anchor says Dean's lockdown line before Dean does. The
last edition arrives only after the ending, and it is the first one dated
Monday. Unless you walked away, in which case it is still Sunday. In Ending D
the page is blank.

**The phone, the horror layer:**

- **The camera.** It shows the echoes of the dead.
- **Photos you didn't take.** They appear in the gallery, framed from where someone was standing.
- **Selfies.** They sometimes catch something behind you. After you meet Chuck, every old selfie has him in it.
- **The payphone.** It rings once an act.
- **Your father's call.** It turns into Chuck doing the voice.
- **Static.** A pocket radio's hiss rises when the fold's things come close.

**Indie touchstones:** Fatal Frame (the camera), P.T. and Visage (the hall
that grows, the looping road), Silent Hill (the fog, the radio static), Doki
Doki and Inscryption (the writer in the machine, a meta ending that deletes
your files), and Mouthwashing (messages you trusted, re-ordered).

**Character arcs, as written into the endings:**

- **Dean** learns that refusing to give up is not the same as refusing to listen.
- **Sam** learns that sacrificing himself is not automatically the compassionate choice.
- **Alistair** is never secretly lying. He remembers every one of their names, and the brothers can argue with his premises rather than dismiss him.
- **Crowley** helps only where it protects his own system.
- **Castiel** acts without certainty.
- **Chuck** mistakes authorship for ownership.

## 9. Seven more cases (1.4)

These cases are built on the shared systems. Each one answers a small question and raises a bigger one. `docs/CASEMAP.md` has the full dependency map.

- **The Dead Man on the Radio** (Act I). Dispatch takes calls from people who haven't died yet, and once from the sheriff, who is alive in the room. Under every call, someone is typing. You can stop the driverless car before 2:23, and if you do, the danger moves onto someone else unless you deal with what's causing it. If you keep the tapes, the one labelled AFTER plays Dean saying "Sam?" in Act III.
- **The Man Who Came Back Wrong** (Act II). Glenn Reed walks out of the fog. His prints, scars and memories are all right, except the details nobody was watching: three knocks instead of two, a loopless g, his mother's missing face. He remembers coffee with Ray in mid-October. The rite finds every seam in him and can't say what they mean.
- **The Inventory of Things That Never Existed** (Act II). Five objects on Earl's step, priced in pennies. Some belonged to people the records dropped. ## 13. As built: two players, Chapter One (1.8)

**The design test.** If one player could gather all the evidence, make every decision and understand the whole mystery without the other, the multiplayer isn't doing its job. Everything below is there to make the second investigator change what the pair can find.

**Roles** (`src/61d_coop_game.js`).
- **The Observer:**
  - reads the county register properly
  - sees edited case-file entries
  - gets the pattern read on the board
  - sees which theories the shared deductions support
- **The Interrogator:**
  - gets +1 trust with every witness
  - is the only one Halvorsen will talk to
  - gets the gut read that rules a theory out
  - gets the "read him" options in dialogue
- Roles are chosen at creation, never by the host's say-so later, and are locked per seat.

**Information** (`coopScan`, `noteMine`, `shareNote`).
- Clues of kind doc, place, photo, note or record auto-share when found.
- Talk and sound clues, visions, and the 2:40 message go to `priv.<seat>.notes`.
- Sharing a note puts its clue in `shared.clues`. A deduction that uses a private note shares the note.

**Joint choices** (`JOINT`, `jointVote`, `jointTick`).
- Round one is sealed.
- If the answers match, the choice is committed. If they differ, round two is the debate: stand by your answer or take your partner's.
- If you still differ, the choice is contested.
- Theories are joint choices with id `th-<case>`, so every case with a theory works this way in co-op.
- Results are written once with compare-and-set (`shared.final.<id>`, `expect:null`), and each client applies them exactly once.

**The Rot** (`src/61f_rot.js`).
- *Why are people dying, and why do the records disagree?*
- **Divide.** The register is in the library (the Observer's discovery: page 0114 is cut, and the stub reads "— 14 Mar — 9 yrs —"). Halvorsen is at the Velvet Hour bar (the Interrogator's: a girl sent home in March, and a man from Delaware who made him cut her page). Lilith waits in the motel lobby.
- **Compare.** `first` (stub + confession) is the deduction neither can make alone. `named` needs Sarah's index card. `motive`, `dust` and `clock` support the three theories:
  - **ghost**: the clock deduction. Plausible.
  - **neglect**: the dust deduction. Plausible, and incomplete.
  - **cover-up**: the first death, the motive and the dust. True.
- **Test: the Fracture.** It starts when the register is on the shared file and both players are online.
  - Stage 1: the Observer sees Sarah at the desk and blood on it. The Interrogator sees an empty room and a clean desk.
  - Desk claims that contradict move it to stage 2: the views swap. The clock, the register's page count and the archive door are claims you can both verify.
  - With two verified claims, the joint question *What in the library is real?* resolves it.
  - The 2:40 message appears once to whoever is in the library while their partner isn't. It's written by the game, recorded in the recipient's notebook, and revealed to both in the chronicle when the room is settled.
- **Consequences.**
  - *What do you tell Sarah?* (joint). Telling her together works only if both brothers have earned it: the sum over both of her trust plus safety must be at least 4. Then she gives you her index card. Otherwise the archive doesn't survive the night.
  - *What you do with what you know* (joint) closes the chapter:
    - **Seven**: the cover-up theory, and the doctor exposed or protected.
    - **Six**: an incomplete theory; or burying it; or publishing a confession Lilith drew out, which he recants.
    - **Two files**: a contested theory or a contested decision.

**Relationships** (`src/61e_rel.js`).
- `rel[a][b][var]` holds deltas over `REL_BASE`, clamped to ±10.
- Writes go through `relEvent`. In co-op only the player whose brother is in the pair can write it, and a brother's own feelings only by his own player.
- The brothers have no desire line, enforced by both the authority and the server.
- `relReact` picks a line from where things stand. Sarah's opening line after the Fracture has six variants, depending on her resentment, your influence, her safety and trust, and your understanding.
- The board's People tab shows your own side in words. Theirs shows only once you understand them.

**Lilith.**
- New, in the motel lobby, in co-op.
- Accepting her help raises your influence and costs her respect for you. Refusing and saying why earns it.
- Her gift opens Halvorsen to either role. The confession is flagged in the ledger as not freely given, and it doesn't survive publication.
- She never touches the brothers' bond.

**The protocol** (`src/61a_coop_core.js`, `server/coop_server.py`).
- Operations are `set`, `inc`, `push`, plus host-only `invite`, `revoke`, `close` and `reopen`.
- Each is checked, stamped with a version, persisted, acknowledged and broadcast. Private ones go only to their owner.
- Clients keep a replica and an outbox. Duplicates are recognised by operation id.
- A 5-second heartbeat finds dead links.
